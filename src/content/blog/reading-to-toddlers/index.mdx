You imagined the cozy scene: a soft lamp, a child nestled in your lap, a story
read cover to cover in a calm voice. Then you had an actual toddler, who turns
three pages at once, wanders off mid-sentence, and demands the same book about a
digger four hundred times in a row. Here is the reframe that fixes everything:
none of that is reading going wrong. That *is* reading, at this age.

The goal of reading to a toddler is not to finish the book. It's to build a
warm, everyday association between you, your voice, and pages full of words.
Comprehension and stamina come later, on their own, if the association is good.
So stop measuring the wrong thing.

## Let them break the rules of the book

A toddler treats a book as an object to be operated, not a narrative to be
received. Good. Follow their lead.

- **Skip to the page they want.** If they flip to the dog, read the dog. You
  are not spoiling the ending; there is no ending they're tracking.
- **Read the pictures, not just the text.** "Look — he's hiding behind the
  tree. Where did the cat go?" Pointing and naming builds vocabulary faster than
  reading the printed sentence verbatim.
- **Let them turn the pages, even too fast.** Turning pages is a motor skill and
  a sign of engagement. A book read in twenty seconds because they raced through
  it still counts.

The child who "won't sit through a book" is often just a child who wants to
*drive*. Hand them the wheel.

## Why the same book, again and again

The digger book, the four hundredth time, is doing something invisible.
Repetition is how a toddler moves a story from "new and effortful" to "known and
predictable," and predictability is deeply satisfying to a small person with
very little control over their day. Each reread, they notice a new detail, or
they fill in a word before you say it, or they "read" it back to you from
memory — which is early literacy, live, in your lap.

So when you are quietly losing your mind over the digger, remember: they are not
bored, and they are not stuck. They are studying. Try leaving a familiar
sentence unfinished — "the digger goes up, up, ___" — and let them supply the
last word. That pause is where a lot of the learning happens.

## Talk more than you read

The single most useful thing you can do with a book is treat it as a
conversation starter rather than a script. Researchers call this dialogic
reading, but you already know how to do it — you just ask questions and follow
where the child goes.

A few prompts that work with almost any book:

1. **Point and label.** "That's a tractor. What color is it?"
2. **Relate it out.** "We saw a truck like that this morning, didn't we?"
3. **Ask what's next.** "Uh oh — what do you think she'll do?"
4. **Let them ask back.** When they point and grunt, answer as if it were a
   full question, because to them it is one.

You will get through fewer words on the page and far more words in the air. The
air is where language is learned.

## Make books ordinary and everywhere

Children read more when books are furniture, not an event. A basket of board
books on the floor where they play. A couple in the car. One in the bag you take
everywhere. When a book is always within reach, reading becomes something that
happens twelve times a day for ninety seconds, which adds up to more than one
heroic bedtime session you're both too tired for anyway.

And keep bedtime books boring on purpose. The bedtime story should be calm and
familiar — a wind-down, not a party. Save the loud, interactive, lift-the-flap
chaos for the middle of the day when you actually want them stirred up.

## When they wander off

They will. Keep reading anyway, in a warm voice, for another sentence or two.
Toddlers are famous for "leaving" an activity while still listening from across
the room, and they often circle back. If they don't, close the book without a
fuss. A story abandoned at page three with no drama is a story they'll happily
pick up again tomorrow. A story turned into a battle is one they learn to avoid.

The still, silent, attentive child on the reading-poster does not exist at this
age, and chasing that image only makes you both miserable. Aim instead for
loud, interrupted, repetitive, and close. That's the version that actually
grows a reader.
