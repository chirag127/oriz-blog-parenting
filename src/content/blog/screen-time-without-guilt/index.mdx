Most screen-time advice starts with a number and ends with a bad feeling. You
read "no more than one hour a day," you glance at the tablet that has clearly
been on for ninety minutes, and you file the whole thing under Things I Am
Failing At. Let's put the stopwatch down for a minute.

The research that scared everyone was mostly about *background* television —
a screen playing to nobody in particular, all day, drowning out the talk and
play that small children actually learn from. That is worth avoiding. But a
child watching a good show with you, or video-calling a grandparent, or looking
up why the moon changes shape, is not the same activity at all. The minutes are
identical. The value is not.

So instead of counting minutes, sort by three questions.

## What is on the screen?

Not all screen time is one thing. A slow, narrated nature program is closer to
a picture book than it is to an auto-playing feed of six-second clips. When you
choose the *content*, you are choosing the pace of your child's attention for
the next half hour.

A rough sorting that has held up in our house:

- **Green light** — things you'd happily watch together: a story, a
  sing-along, a "how is bread made" video, a call with family.
- **Yellow light** — games and shows that are fine in small doses but tend to
  end in a meltdown when switched off. Set the ending before you start.
- **Red light** — anything with an infinite feed and autoplay. The design goal
  of that software is to prevent a natural stopping point, which is exactly the
  skill you are trying to teach.

You don't need to police this like a censor. You just need to know which bucket
today's choice is in.

## When does it happen?

Screens are least harmful when they sit inside a predictable slot and most
harmful when they become the tool you reach for to end every hard moment. A
tablet handed over the instant a child is bored teaches boredom is an
emergency. A show at the same point each day — after the bath, before dinner —
becomes a boring, ordinary part of the routine, which is precisely what you
want it to be.

Two timing rules do most of the work:

1. **Not the first thing, not the last thing.** Screens right after waking make
   the morning harder; screens right before bed push sleep later and make it
   lighter. Bookend the day with almost anything else.
2. **Name the end before the start.** "Two episodes, then we turn it off and do
   puzzles" is a promise you can keep. "We'll see" is a negotiation you will
   lose.

## Who is there?

The single biggest upgrade to screen time costs nothing: sit down for some of
it. When you watch together, you pause, you point, you ask "why do you think he
did that?" — and a passive activity turns into a shared one. You also see what
your child is actually absorbing, which is hard to do from the kitchen.

You cannot co-view everything, and you shouldn't have to. But if a show is
worth watching, it's worth watching *with* them at least sometimes. That's how
a screen stops being a babysitter and starts being one more thing you do
together.

## The turn-off, without the war

The hard part was never the watching. It's the stopping. A few things that
lower the temperature:

- Give a warning that's an *event*, not a countdown: "one more song, then
  screens go to sleep too." Machines needing rest is a story kids accept
  surprisingly well.
- Have the next thing ready. A child leaving a screen for nothing will fight;
  a child leaving a screen for a snack and blocks already on the table often
  won't.
- Let the device live somewhere, not in a pocket. When the tablet has a
  "home" on a high shelf, putting it away is a physical, final act rather than
  an argument about whether it's really off.

## The part nobody says out loud

Some days the screen will run long because you are sick, or working, or simply
out of road. That is not a moral failure; it is a Tuesday. A steady pattern
across a month matters far more than any single afternoon. Kids raised by calm,
present adults who occasionally use a screen to survive turn out fine. Kids
raised by anxious, guilt-soaked adults counting every minute are the ones
absorbing the real lesson — that this stuff is scary.

Pick your content, protect the edges of the day, watch some of it together, and
let the rest go. The guilt was never helping anyone learn anything.
